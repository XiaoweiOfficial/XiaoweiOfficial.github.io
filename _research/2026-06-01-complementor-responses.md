excerpt: "First-party complement provision (FPCP) — a platform owner releasing its own complements alongside those of independent complementors, as when Sony publishes in-house games for the PlayStation — is usually read as a competitive move that discourages complementor participation. I develop a category-contingent perspective that foregrounds the demand-spillover effects of FPCP within a platform-category, treating category strength as multidimensional along uniqueness, commercial performance, and quality. When a category is weak on these dimensions, the spillover value of FPCP becomes more consequential and complementors respond more favorably than they otherwise would. Evidence comes from the U.S. console video game industry, 1995–2005, using fixed-effects Poisson models with extensive robustness checks. <br /><br /><i>Dissertation chapter.
